Gwinnett County Announces New Era of “Accountability” for Animal Welfare

GWINNETT COUNTY, GA — On August 4, the Gwinnett County Board of Commissioners voted to restructure the Gwinnett Animal Advisory Council, successfully liberating all seven citizen members from their duties.

This decisive action came shortly after several members committed the ultimate civic offense: attempting to perform their actual job.

The timing was a masterclass in bureaucratic self-preservation.

For years, the Council maintained an exemplary record of quiet compliance. Whenever a member attempted to speak up, administrative gravity had a remarkable way of pulling things back into place.

Last year, for example, a former Council member reported being helpfully reminded by the then-Council Chair that “the role of the Animal Advisory Council was to advocate for the shelter—not the animals.”

Apparently, someone forgot to tell the Animal Advisory Council that animals were part of the assignment.

The former member also reported that the Chair accused her of Code of Ethics violations and explained that the County's Legal Department had asked her to have that meeting.

Nothing restores independent citizen oversight quite like a meeting reportedly requested by County Legal to explain why the citizen doing the overseeing was doing it wrong.

Unfortunately, recent alarming levels of public engagement threatened to upset this carefully maintained equilibrium.

More than 50 residents packed the July 13 meeting, and Council members dared to schedule a special meeting for September 3 to discuss actual shelter reforms.

Questions were being asked.

Operational data was being requested.

Recommendations were moving forward.

County leadership immediately spotted the emergency:

The Advisory Council was becoming far too advisory.

To neutralize the threat, the Board unveiled what it calls a “skills-based council equipped to meet the needs of the moment.”

Translation:

  • Eliminate Direct Citizen Representation: District-based citizen appointees are gone. Broad community representation is out; curated, government-approved “expertise” is in.
  • Self-Auditing Synergy: Instead of individual Commissioners appointing district representatives, candidate recommendations will now flow through Community Services Director Lindsey Jorstad — the head of the same County department that oversees Animal Welfare & Enforcement.
  • Chaired by the Watchdogged: To ensure maximum “alignment,” Animal Welfare & Enforcement Director Katrina Helman will serve as the Council's nonvoting Chair.

This cutting-edge framework gives the department being advised a much more hands-on role in who gets recommended to advise it, while the Animal Welfare director leads the meetings where her own department may be discussed.

As the County explained, this is simply “part of the County’s practice of periodically reviewing its boards.”

Efficiency! Synergy! Self-Oversight!

Commissioner Kirkland Carden took to social media to defend the overhaul, explaining that “accountability and adherence to the law are not optional.”

He alerted the public that former Council members had been “repeatedly overstepping their boundaries” and “interfering with the essential, day-to-day work of our professional staff.”

Among these troubling boundary violations:

  • Insubordinate Curiosity: Requesting records and asking questions about shelter operations.
  • Reckless Passion: Suggesting actual policy reforms instead of approving the minutes and going home.
  • Aggressive Organization: Attempting to hold additional public meetings to discuss public matters.

Commissioner Carden also warned that the Council had triggered “serious red flags from our legal department—including potential violations of the Georgia Open Meetings Act.”

Records have since revealed a glimpse into the horror of this lawless chaos:

A Council member CC'd her colleagues on an email asking staff to correct the meeting location on the County's own public website.

Former Council leadership warned that copying everyone on a website-location fix technically violated the Open Meetings Act.

Animal Welfare Director Katrina Helman later clarified that the email was “not a concern.”

Nevertheless, the damage was done.

The Great CC Crisis of 2026 had pushed local government to the absolute brink.

Thankfully, the Board demonstrated its own pristine commitment to transparency on August 4.

The ordinance dismantling the Council somehow failed to make it onto the advance published agenda.

No problem.

The Board simply added it at the meeting and voted on it that same afternoon.

As Commissioner Carden eloquently put it:

“Our boards must operate with transparency, within their legal authority, and strictly for their intended purpose.”

An inspiring standard.

Commissioner Carden has since assured the public that the County has been “exceptionally transparent,” pointing to press releases, social media updates and town halls.

Residents seeking clarification about the differing explanations for the Council's dissolution are encouraged to consult the press release.

If the press release doesn't answer your question, please consult the press release again.

Exceptional transparency.
Now with unlimited rereads.

With district-based citizen representation eliminated, the department being advised brought considerably closer to the advising, and the public successfully spared from seeing the ordinance on the advance agenda, Gwinnett County looks forward to a quiet, peaceful and thoroughly compliant future in animal welfare.

Future Council members are still warmly encouraged to serve, provided their commitment to animal welfare “does not come at the expense of the law” or become a “hindrance” to County staff.

Terms and conditions may apply.
Excessive advising could result in restructuring.
