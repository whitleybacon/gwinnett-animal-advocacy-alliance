Week Ending October 2, 2026

This week brought developments on several fronts for Gwinnett Animal Advocacy Alliance—from expanding our state-level advocacy, to Gwinnett County publicly acknowledging pet overpopulation, to continued concerns about shelter capacity and animals at risk.

Here's what happened this week.

🏛️ Taking Animal Welfare to the State Level

Our state advocacy work moved forward significantly this week.

On September 29, GAAA met with Georgia House candidate Jennifer Ambler to discuss animal welfare as a state policy issue—including shelter capacity, prevention, enforcement, irresponsible breeding, affordability, and the growing burden placed on local governments and shelters.

The conversation went extremely well, and Jennifer connected us with additional candidates interested in animal welfare issues.

We also finalized GAAA's Georgia General Assembly Animal Welfare Pledge, an 11-point commitment addressing spay/neuter funding, animal cruelty laws and enforcement, repeat offenders, responsible breeding, transparency and data, local-state collaboration, responsiveness to constituents, and public safety and education.

So far, Rep. Marvin Lim, Adam Cleveland, Juan Estrada and Sen. Sally Harrell have signed the pledge.

Next week, we have conversations scheduled with Adam Cleveland and Rep. Akbar Ali as our outreach to candidates and legislators continues.

Our goal isn't simply to discuss what is happening inside one shelter. We are looking at where state policy can help communities across Georgia address the systemic issues contributing to animal welfare challenges.

🐾 Gwinnett Publicly Acknowledges Pet Overpopulation

One of the most significant moments this week came during Gwinnett County Community Services' 2027 business plan presentation.

When asked about one of the biggest challenges facing Animal Welfare, Animal Welfare Division Director Katrina Hellman identified pet overpopulation, describing it as “one of the toughest things that we've been challenged with.”

That acknowledgment matters.

For months, GAAA has emphasized that shelter overcrowding cannot be addressed solely through adoption, fostering and rescue. Those efforts are essential, but they primarily address animals after they have already entered the system.

Long-term progress requires addressing the source of the problem through prevention—including accessible spay/neuter services, responsible pet ownership, effective enforcement and policies that reduce preventable shelter intake.

The presentation also highlighted improvements at the Bill Atkinson Animal Welfare Center and efforts to make volunteer orientation more accessible.

We are encouraged to hear County leadership publicly recognize the overpopulation problem.

The next question is what Gwinnett will do to address its causes.

🐾 Animals at Risk

Last week, 13 dogs faced a September 29 euthanasia deadline at Gwinnett Animal Welfare and all are safe for now.

Today, we received an urgent plea from the shelter for 10 dogs needing rescue commitment, foster placement, or adoption by 5:00 p.m. Wednesday, October 7.

We are glad to see the shelter proactively reaching out for help before the deadline.

At the same time, 10 more dogs facing another placement deadline just days after the previous group of 13 underscores that Gwinnett's capacity problem is far from resolved.

GAAA will continue encouraging the County to use all of its public-facing platforms—not only rescue networks—to make sure residents know when animals are at risk.

If the public does not know animals are at risk, they cannot help.

🐾 The Paw Print Takeaway

This week showed why animal welfare requires work at every level—from state policy and local government to shelter operations and community involvement.

There is progress worth acknowledging, and there are challenges that remain.

GAAA will continue working for prevention, transparency, accountability and meaningful solutions that address the causes—not simply the consequences—of pet overpopulation.
