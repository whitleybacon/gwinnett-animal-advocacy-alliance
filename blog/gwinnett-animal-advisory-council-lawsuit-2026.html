Gwinnett County Faces Lawsuit Following Animal Advisory Council Restructuring

October 15, 2026 · Gwinnett Animal Advocacy Alliance

Public scrutiny of Gwinnett County's animal welfare operations was growing.

Residents were raising concerns about shelter practices, transparency, accountability, and the County's response to the ongoing pet-overpopulation crisis.

Meanwhile, the County's Animal Advisory Council was examining issues that could lead to meaningful improvements in animal welfare.

Council members were working toward recommendations for the Board of Commissioners to consider, including a countywide spay/neuter voucher program, an independent external audit of Gwinnett Animal Welfare, and improved camera coverage and oversight at the shelter.

These were matters being researched and discussed as potential recommendations, not policies the Council had independently enacted or implemented.

Community engagement was also increasing. More than 50 residents attended the Council's July 13 meeting, where discussions remained unfinished. A special meeting was scheduled for September 3 to continue that work.

But that meeting would never take place under the existing Council structure.

On August 4, the Gwinnett County Board of Commissioners voted to dissolve and restructure the seven-member Animal Advisory Council, replacing it with a five-member, skills-based advisory body.

The decision was unanimous.

What was not apparent to residents reviewing the advance public agenda was that the restructuring would be considered that day.

According to County records described in a lawsuit subsequently filed in Gwinnett County Superior Court, the proposal had already been prepared and formally submitted on July 31, four days before the vote.

Yet the restructuring did not appear on the advance agenda. It was added during the August 4 meeting, and commissioners approved it before public comment.

In the weeks that followed, questions emerged about how the proposal originated, why the decision was made when it was, and whether the County had followed the legal requirements governing public meetings and citizen appointments.

Efforts to obtain records identifying who initiated the restructuring and when also encountered privilege-based withholdings.

On September 30, 2026, three individuals filed a lawsuit challenging the process behind the restructuring, the withholding of public records, and the termination of two Council members' appointments.

Gwinnett County was served on October 7, 2026.

A Council Working Toward Recommendations

The Animal Advisory Council's role was to advise the Gwinnett County Board of Commissioners on animal welfare matters.

That role included examining concerns, discussing possible solutions, and developing recommendations for commissioners to evaluate.

At the time of the restructuring, Council members had been exploring several issues of significant public interest.

  • Countywide spay/neuter voucher program: Exploring a prevention-focused initiative to help residents access affordable sterilization services and reduce unwanted litters.
  • Independent external audit: Discussing a recommendation for an outside review of Gwinnett Animal Welfare operations and practices.
  • Shelter cameras and oversight: Considering improvements to camera coverage and accountability at the animal shelter.

These proposals had not been independently enacted by the Council. They were subjects of research, discussion, and potential recommendations to the Board.

The distinction matters because Commissioner Kirkland Carden later publicly raised allegations that Council members had exceeded their authority.

The Council's advisory role did not give it independent authority to implement the proposals under discussion. Its ability to research and recommend changes was distinct from the Board's authority to approve or reject them.

Whether particular conduct exceeded the Council's authority is a separate question that cannot be resolved solely by identifying the subjects the Council was discussing.

The Council was working on potential recommendations for commissioners to consider — not independently implementing those reforms.

How the Restructuring Unfolded

  1. July 13 — More Than 50 Residents Attend

    Residents attended the Animal Advisory Council meeting amid growing interest in animal welfare issues. Business remained unfinished, and a special meeting was scheduled for September 3.

  2. July 31 — Restructuring Proposal Submitted

    According to the complaint, a proposal containing a justification memorandum, resolution, and replacement ordinance was formally submitted for the August 4 meeting.

  3. August 4, 10:27 a.m. — Request to Add Item

    Community Services Director Lindsey Jorstad emailed commissioners requesting that the restructuring be added to that afternoon's 2 p.m. meeting.

  4. August 4 — Commissioners Vote

    Commissioner Jasper Watkins III moved to add the restructuring to the agenda.

    The motion passed 4–1, with Commissioner Ben Ku opposed.

    The Board subsequently approved the restructuring by a 5–0 vote. Public comment followed the vote.

  5. After August 4 — Council Members Notified

    Sitting Council members received notifications indicating that their appointments had concluded.

  6. September 30 — Lawsuit Filed

    Three individuals filed a complaint in Gwinnett County Superior Court, raising claims under Georgia's Open Meetings Act, Open Records Act, and concerning the legal status of two Council appointments.

  7. October 7 — County Served

    The Gwinnett County Sheriff's Office served the County with the lawsuit.

Why Was the Council Restructured?

The County's explanations for the restructuring have themselves become part of the public discussion.

Before the August 4 meeting, the County prepared an information sheet addressing anticipated questions about the proposal.

One question asked whether the restructuring was because of concerns with the current Council.

"Is this because of concerns with the current Council?"

The County's prepared answer: "No."

The accompanying explanation described the restructuring as a periodic review reflecting Gwinnett's growth and evolving community needs.

After the restructuring, however, Commissioner Kirkland Carden publicly raised concerns about Council members allegedly exceeding their authority and subverting public meeting laws.

Commissioner Ben Ku later cited staff safety concerns as contributing to the suddenness of the restructuring.

According to the complaint, records concerning a referenced March 2026 threat identified an individual who was not a member of the Animal Advisory Council.

The County also reported finding no responsive communications, within the requested period, connecting that threat to the Council or the restructuring.

The prepared explanation and later public statements leave questions about the timeline and circumstances surrounding the decision.

On October 15, The North Gwinnett Voice published an investigative report examining County records, the restructuring timeline, and the explanations offered for the decision.

The lawsuit's legal claims concern the public meeting process, the withholding of records, and the legal status of two Council appointments. It does not establish why individual officials supported the restructuring.

What Does the Lawsuit Allege?

The complaint raises three principal legal claims.

1. Georgia Open Meetings Act

Georgia's Open Meetings Act requires public agencies to make available an agenda identifying matters expected to come before them.

The law allows certain matters to be added during a meeting when they become necessary to address during that meeting.

The plaintiffs allege that the Council restructuring was already an expected agenda item because the proposal had been developed and formally submitted four days earlier.

The complaint further alleges that no intervening event or explanation establishing the need for same-day consideration was identified at the August 4 meeting.

If the restructuring was prepared days in advance, why wasn't it included on the advance public agenda?

The plaintiffs ask the Court to determine whether the County violated the Open Meetings Act and whether the August 4 action should be declared nonbinding to the extent authorized by law.

2. Georgia Open Records Act

Following the vote, records requests sought to identify when the restructuring originated, who initiated it, and how the proposal developed.

According to the complaint, the County acknowledged that responsive records existed identifying when and by whom the restructuring was initiated.

Those records were withheld in their entirety under claims of attorney-client privilege.

Additional drafts, redlines, and related records were also withheld under attorney-client privilege or attorney work product.

The plaintiffs do not dispute the protection of legitimate privileged legal communications.

Instead, they seek an in camera review, meaning a private examination by the Court, to determine whether the claimed exemptions were properly applied.

They also seek disclosure of reasonably separable, nonexempt information, including factual or administrative details where disclosure is required.

The complaint describes an instance in which identifying information about an email was released while the email's body remained withheld.

The question is not whether legitimate legal advice may be protected. It is whether all of the information being withheld is legally exempt from disclosure.

3. Legal Status of Two Council Appointments

The third claim concerns the District 1 and District 2 Council appointees.

Neither had resigned before the August 4 restructuring.

Following the vote, County staff notified sitting members that their terms had concluded.

The complaint alleges that neither the August 4 resolution nor the replacement ordinance expressly terminated those appointments.

It also notes that the meeting minutes do not reflect a separate Board vote removing the sitting members.

The replacement ordinance contains provisions addressing removal by majority vote of the Board.

Did the Board lawfully terminate the appointments, or were they subsequently treated as terminated without the required Board action?

The plaintiffs ask the Court to determine the legal effect of the restructuring and subsequent notices.

If the appointments were not lawfully terminated, they seek appropriate relief, including restoration.

What Are the Plaintiffs Asking the Court to Do?

  • Review the agenda process: Determine whether the County violated Georgia's Open Meetings Act.
  • Determine the legal effect of the August 4 action: Consider whether the restructuring should be declared nonbinding and whether injunctive relief is warranted.
  • Review Council appointments: Determine whether two appointments were lawfully terminated and, if not, whether they should be restored.
  • Examine withheld records: Conduct a private judicial review of materials withheld under attorney-client privilege and attorney work product.
  • Require disclosure where warranted: Order production of nonexempt records and reasonably separable nonexempt information.

These are remedies requested in the complaint. The Court has not yet ruled on the merits of the claims.

The Questions That Remain

Why was a restructuring proposal prepared days earlier omitted from the advance public agenda?

Who initiated the restructuring, and when did the process begin?

Which responsive records, or portions of records, are legally exempt from public disclosure?

How should residents understand the County's prepared explanation alongside subsequent statements about Council conduct and safety?

Were two sitting Council members' appointments lawfully terminated?

Why This Matters to Gwinnett Residents

This case is about more than the structure of one advisory council.

It raises questions about how local government makes decisions, how much advance notice residents receive, and whether the public has an opportunity to participate before decisions are finalized.

Citizen advisory councils provide a channel for community perspectives, research, and recommendations to reach elected officials.

In Gwinnett, residents have spent considerable time raising concerns about shelter overcrowding, pet overpopulation, transparency, and prevention-focused solutions.

The Council was considering recommendations on issues directly connected to those concerns.

When a public advisory body is restructured, the process matters.

When records about how that decision originated are requested, compliance with the Open Records Act matters.

And when residents are appointed to serve on a public advisory body, the legal process for ending those appointments matters.

Transparency isn't simply telling residents what government decided.

It's allowing them to understand and participate in the process before the decision is made.

The three plaintiffs have asked the Court to examine these issues under Georgia law.

GAAA will continue advocating for meaningful animal welfare reform, public participation, and accountability in Gwinnett County.

The animals deserve a voice. The public deserves accountability.

About This Article & the Lawsuit

This article summarizes allegations and requests for relief in a lawsuit filed September 30, 2026, in Gwinnett County Superior Court.

Case number: 26-A-08947-9

The lawsuit was filed by three individuals in their personal capacities. Gwinnett Animal Advocacy Alliance (GAAA) is not a plaintiff.

The allegations have not been adjudicated, and no court has determined that Gwinnett County violated the law.

The discussion of the Council's work describes potential recommendations under consideration, not reforms independently enacted by the Council.

This article is intended to explain the documented background and issues raised in the public court filing, not to predict the outcome of the case.
