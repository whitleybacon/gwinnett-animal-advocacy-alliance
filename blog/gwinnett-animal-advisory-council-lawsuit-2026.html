Gwinnett County Faces Lawsuit Over Animal Advisory Council Restructuring

October 16, 2026 | Gwinnett Animal Advocacy Alliance

For years, animal advocates have raised concerns about animal welfare in Gwinnett County, calling for greater transparency, accountability, and meaningful improvements to shelter operations.

From overcrowding and animal deaths to shelter practices, volunteer participation, and the ongoing pet-overpopulation crisis, advocates have repeatedly asked County leadership to address longstanding problems.

More recently, several members of Gwinnett County's Animal Advisory Council began bringing some of those same concerns into the County's own advisory process.

Council members were requesting information, questioning shelter operations, and developing potential recommendations for the Board of Commissioners to consider.

By July 13, 2026, that effort had gained significant public attention. More than 50 animal advocates and residents attended the Council meeting, a substantial increase from the handful who typically attended.

The agenda reflected a level of activity that advocates had long hoped to see. Council members were examining proposals involving an independent external audit, additional shelter cameras, a countywide spay/neuter voucher and microchip program, and more detailed reporting on shelter operations and animal deaths.

The Council did not complete its agenda that evening. Members unanimously scheduled a special meeting for September 3 to continue their work.

But less than a month later, the Gwinnett County Board of Commissioners dissolved and restructured the Council.

On August 4, commissioners voted to replace the seven-member Animal Advisory Council with a five-member, skills-based advisory body.

The restructuring had been prepared and formally submitted days earlier, yet it was not included on the advance public agenda. Instead, commissioners added it during the meeting and approved it before public comment.

What followed was a series of questions about how the restructuring originated, why it was handled so abruptly, the explanations offered by County officials, and the legal status of sitting Council members.

Efforts to obtain records identifying who initiated the restructuring and when also encountered privilege-based withholdings.

On September 30, three individuals filed a lawsuit in Gwinnett County Superior Court challenging the County's actions under Georgia's Open Meetings Act, Open Records Act, and the legal requirements governing Council appointments.

What Was Happening Before the Restructuring?

The former Animal Advisory Council included citizen members appointed through the County's district-based appointment process. Its purpose was to provide advice and recommendations to the Board of Commissioners on animal welfare matters.

According to former Council members who spoke with GAAA, efforts to advance meaningful recommendations had previously encountered resistance within the Council.

That began to change as several members became more active in requesting information, introducing proposals, and pushing for greater transparency and accountability.

The July 13 agenda included a wide range of issues, from shelter operations and animal outcomes to potential policy changes.

Among the proposals was additional camera coverage in areas housing animals. With shelter construction already underway, the Council unanimously voted, 7–0, to recommend that shelter management obtain a bid to evaluate the feasibility of adding bird's-eye-view cameras as part of the ongoing project.

Members also discussed proposals to recommend an independent external audit of Gwinnett Animal Welfare and a countywide spay/neuter voucher and microchip program for the Board of Commissioners to consider. Both proposals were unanimously tabled for further discussion.

Other agenda items sought more information about euthanasia, unassisted animal deaths, kitten deaths, enrichment activities, volunteer hours, rescue outreach, and the visibility of animals needing urgent placement.

These discussions were significant because they addressed issues advocates had been raising for years.

Importantly, the Council was working toward recommendations, not independently implementing reforms.

The camera vote was a recommendation to investigate feasibility, not approval to install cameras. The external audit and spay/neuter proposals remained under consideration.

The Council's advisory role did not give it independent authority to authorize audits, direct shelter operations, or commit County funds.

A Dispute Over the Council's Authority and Public Meetings

Following the August 4 restructuring vote, Commissioner Kirkland Carden publicly accused Council members of exceeding their authority and "actively subverting public meeting laws," claiming they had amended their bylaws to meet privately without County staff.

However, the meeting minutes show that the Council approved additional meetings without shelter staff—not meetings closed to the public. The proposal followed concerns about requiring staff to attend more meetings, and the previous Council ordinance did not require shelter staff to attend Council meetings. Furthermore, the County Attorney was present at the July 13 meeting and raised no concerns about the proposed meetings without staff. Those meetings would still be subject to Georgia's Open Meetings Act.

GAAA requested records supporting Carden's allegations. The County ultimately produced two emails concerning Council email practices, but neither documented an unlawful closed meeting or a warning about the July 13 proposal. GAAA also questioned Carden's claims of unauthorized audits and oversight, noting that members were discussing potential recommendations—not independently exercising those powers.

Despite follow-up inquiries, GAAA received no documented explanation identifying the specific Council conduct that allegedly warranted restructuring or confirming whether County Legal had warned members about such conduct during a public meeting.

Different Explanations for the Same Decision

Carden's public allegations raised another question: how did they relate to the County's original explanation for restructuring the Council?

Before the August 4 vote, the County had prepared an information sheet addressing anticipated questions.

One question asked whether the restructuring was prompted by concerns about the current Council.

The County's prepared answer was: "No."

The written justification instead emphasized modernization, professional expertise, and improved coordination and collaboration.

Yet Carden subsequently cited alleged misconduct and members exceeding their authority as reasons for the restructuring. Commissioner Ben Ku offered another explanation for its abrupt timing, stating that staff-safety concerns contributed to the suddenness of the decision.

GAAA sought records concerning a referenced threat involving shelter staff. Community Services Director Lindsey Jorstad confirmed that the individual associated with the threat was NOT an Animal Advisory Council member. Records later identified the incident as a March 17, 2026 threat involving a dispute over a dog named Stella, months before the July 13 Council meeting.

Taken together, the County's prepared explanation, Carden's allegations, and Ku's statements raise questions about what prompted the restructuring and why it was handled so abruptly.

The August 4 Decision: Prepared in Advance, Added That Day

Records obtained through Open Records Act requests confirmed that the restructuring proposal was prepared and submitted by July 31, 2026, four days before the Board's vote. The proposal replaced the seven-member, district-based Council with five specialized seats, with candidates recommended by the Community Services Director and the Animal Welfare & Enforcement Director serving as nonvoting chair.

GAAA has raised concerns about the potential conflict of interest created by this structure. The same County leadership responsible for overseeing shelter operations would now have significant influence over the composition and leadership of the Council tasked with advising on those operations. This raises questions about whether the restructured Council can provide independent oversight and recommendations, particularly when its findings or proposals may be critical of County management.

Despite being prepared in advance, the restructuring was not included on the publicly posted agenda. Instead, it was added during the August 4 meeting by a 4–1 vote, with Commissioner Ben Ku opposed. The Board then approved the restructuring 5–0, before public comment.

Georgia's Open Meetings Act generally requires public agencies to include anticipated business on their advance meeting agendas. Although certain items may be added during a meeting, the law provides an exception for matters that become necessary to address during the meeting.

This is central to the lawsuit's Georgia Open Meetings Act claim. The plaintiffs argue that because the restructuring proposal had been prepared and submitted days earlier, it should have appeared on the public agenda. According to the complaint, no intervening event or necessity for same-day consideration was identified. Commissioner Ku later explained that he opposed adding the item because he wanted more time to review it.

The Records the Public Still Hasn't Seen

Following the vote, GAAA sought records to determine who initiated the restructuring, when it began, and how the proposal developed. Although the County produced the restructuring documents, they did not reveal the proposal's origins.

GAAA submitted a narrower request seeking only identifying information, explicitly excluding privileged legal advice. The County acknowledged that responsive records existed but withheld them in full, citing attorney-client privilege. Additional drafts and communications were also withheld under privilege claims.

Georgia's Open Records Act generally requires agencies to release reasonably separable, nonexempt information, even when other portions of a record are protected. GAAA requested such information, but the County maintained its position. In a separate request, the County had demonstrated that separation was possible by releasing an email's identifying details while withholding its content.

The lawsuit does not seek disclosure of legitimate privileged legal advice. Instead, it asks the Court to privately review the withheld records and determine whether the exemptions were properly applied and whether nonexempt information must be released.

Did the Restructuring Legally End the Existing Appointments?

A separate legal question arose immediately after the August 4 vote. Community Services Director Lindsey Jorstad notified sitting Council members that their appointments had ended as part of the restructuring.

However, the previous ordinance stated that members in good standing would continue serving until reappointed or replaced by the Board of Commissioners. The new ordinance retained that provision and explicitly required a majority Board vote to remove members. According to the complaint, neither the restructuring resolution nor the new ordinance expressly terminated the sitting members' appointments, and the meeting minutes reflect no separate vote removing them.

Two former Council members now challenge whether the restructuring vote itself lawfully ended their appointments, or whether the subsequent staff notices effectively removed them without the required Board action. The lawsuit asks the Court to resolve that question.

What the Lawsuit Asks the Court to Decide

The September 30 lawsuit brings three distinct legal challenges arising from these events.

1. Georgia Open Meetings Act

Whether the County violated advance agenda requirements by adding and approving a restructuring proposal that had been prepared and formally submitted days before the meeting.

The plaintiffs ask the Court to declare the August 4 restructuring legally nonbinding and, to the extent authorized by law, prevent the County from continuing to implement it unless the matter is reconsidered in compliance with Georgia's Open Meetings Act.

2. Georgia Open Records Act

Whether the County properly withheld records under attorney-client privilege and attorney work product, and whether any reasonably separable nonexempt information must be produced.

The complaint seeks an in camera review, allowing the judge to examine the withheld records privately before deciding whether disclosure is required.

3. Council Appointments

Whether the August 4 action lawfully terminated two sitting Council members' appointments, or whether subsequent staff notices treated those appointments as ended without sufficient Board action.

The affected former members seek a judicial declaration and, if warranted, restoration to the Council.

These are claims and remedies requested by the plaintiffs. The allegations have not been adjudicated.

Why This Matters to Gwinnett Residents

The controversy surrounding the Animal Advisory Council is about more than the membership of one advisory body. For years, animal advocates have urged Gwinnett County to address longstanding animal welfare problems and pursue prevention-focused solutions.

The timing of the restructuring raises important questions. Just as public pressure was mounting, more residents were attending Council meetings, and several Council members were actively developing recommendations to address those concerns, the Board voted to dismantle and restructure the Council. The decision came just three weeks after the July 13 meeting, interrupting the Council's work under its existing structure.

County officials subsequently offered different explanations involving modernization, alleged Council misconduct, and staff safety, leaving questions about what prompted the decision and why it was made so abruptly.

The lawsuit now asks the Court to determine whether the County complied with legal requirements governing public meetings, access to records, and Council appointments.

Regardless of the outcome, these questions matter to every resident who expects government decisions to be made through a transparent, accountable, and lawful process.

What began as growing demands for transparency and accountability in Gwinnett animal welfare has now raised questions about transparency and accountability in the County's own decision-making.

GAAA will continue following the case and advocating for meaningful animal welfare improvements, public participation, and government accountability.
