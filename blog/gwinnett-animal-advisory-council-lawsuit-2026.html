Gwinnett County Faces Lawsuit Over Animal Advisory Council Restructuring

October 15, 2026 | Gwinnett Animal Advocacy Alliance

For years, animal advocates have raised concerns about animal welfare in Gwinnett County, calling for greater transparency, accountability, and meaningful improvements to shelter operations.

From overcrowding and animal deaths to shelter practices, volunteer participation, and the ongoing pet-overpopulation crisis, advocates have repeatedly asked County leadership to address longstanding problems.

More recently, several members of Gwinnett County's Animal Advisory Council began bringing some of those same concerns into the County's own advisory process.

Council members were requesting information, questioning shelter operations, and developing potential recommendations for the Board of Commissioners to consider.

By July 13, 2026, that effort had gained significant public attention. More than 50 animal advocates and residents attended the Council meeting, a substantial increase from the handful who typically attended.

The agenda reflected a level of activity that advocates had long hoped to see. Council members were examining proposals involving an independent external audit, additional shelter cameras, a countywide spay/neuter voucher and microchip program, and more detailed reporting on shelter operations and animal deaths.

The Council did not complete its agenda that evening. Members unanimously scheduled a special meeting for September 3 to continue their work.

But less than a month later, the Gwinnett County Board of Commissioners dissolved and restructured the Council.

On August 4, commissioners voted to replace the seven-member Animal Advisory Council with a five-member, skills-based advisory body.

The restructuring had been prepared and formally submitted days earlier, yet it was not included on the advance public agenda. Instead, commissioners added it during the meeting and approved it before public comment.

What followed was a series of questions about how the restructuring originated, why it was handled so abruptly, the explanations offered by County officials, and the legal status of sitting Council members.

Efforts to obtain records identifying who initiated the restructuring and when also encountered privilege-based withholdings.

On September 30, three individuals filed a lawsuit in Gwinnett County Superior Court challenging the County's actions under Georgia's Open Meetings Act, Open Records Act, and the legal requirements governing Council appointments.

Gwinnett County was served on October 7.

What Was Happening Before the Restructuring?

The former Animal Advisory Council included citizen members appointed through the County's district-based appointment process. Its purpose was to provide advice and recommendations to the Board of Commissioners on animal welfare matters.

According to former Council members who spoke with GAAA, efforts to advance meaningful recommendations had previously encountered resistance within the Council.

That began to change as several members became more active in requesting information, introducing proposals, and pushing for greater transparency and accountability.

The July 13 agenda included a wide range of issues, from shelter operations and animal outcomes to potential policy changes.

Among the proposals was additional camera coverage in areas housing animals. The Council unanimously voted, 7–0, to recommend that shelter management seek a bid to evaluate the feasibility of bird's-eye-view cameras.

Members also discussed an independent external audit of Gwinnett Animal Welfare. Rather than advancing that recommendation immediately, the Council unanimously postponed consideration until members could review an earlier County audit.

A proposed countywide spay/neuter voucher and microchip program was similarly deferred for further discussion.

Other agenda items sought more information about euthanasia, unassisted animal deaths, kitten deaths, enrichment activities, volunteer hours, rescue outreach, and the visibility of animals needing urgent placement.

These discussions were significant because they addressed issues advocates had been raising for years.

Importantly, the Council was working toward recommendations, not independently implementing reforms.

The camera vote was a recommendation to investigate feasibility, not approval to install cameras. The external audit and spay/neuter proposals remained under consideration.

The Council's advisory role did not give it independent authority to authorize audits, direct shelter operations, or commit County funds.

That distinction became important after Commissioner Kirkland Carden publicly accused Council members of exceeding their authority.

A Dispute Over the Council's Authority and Public Meetings

One of the issues discussed on July 13 involved the Council's meeting schedule.

Members considered holding additional meetings between their regular quarterly meetings so they could continue working through outstanding business.

According to the meeting minutes, an initial proposal to meet monthly without shelter staff failed 3–4.

A revised proposal to meet every other month, without shelter staff and at a location to be determined, passed 4–1, with two abstentions.

GAAA understands that the discussion about staff attendance arose, in part, because some Council members had expressed concerns about requiring County employees to attend additional meetings.

The meeting minutes do not describe a proposal to exclude the public, eliminate public notice, or hold meetings behind closed doors.

Nevertheless, following the restructuring, Commissioner Carden publicly characterized the Council's action differently.

In an August 7 Facebook post and subsequent comments, Carden accused former Council members of exceeding their authority and interfering with County staff.

He further alleged that the Council had amended its bylaws to meet privately while excluding County staff, accusing members of "actively subverting public meeting laws."

The July 13 minutes describe meetings without shelter staff, not meetings closed to the public.

The distinction is important. A public advisory body does not cease to be subject to Georgia's Open Meetings Act simply because shelter staff are not present.

The Act's requirements concerning public access, notice, and other procedures remain applicable.

GAAA's review of the former Council's governing rules also did not identify a requirement that shelter staff attend every Council meeting.

County legal counsel was present at Council meetings and had provided guidance on procedural questions on other occasions.

GAAA therefore questioned why, if the proposal raised legal concerns, Council members had not been advised of those concerns when the motion was considered.

To examine Carden's allegations, GAAA submitted an Open Records Act request seeking documents concerning alleged Open Meetings Act violations, legal warnings or guidance, and whether those concerns influenced the restructuring.

The County initially reported finding no responsive records. It later corrected that response and produced two emails concerning Council email practices.

Those emails included guidance cautioning members against discussing official business or policy through group email.

They did not document an unlawful closed Council meeting or a formal warning about the July 13 proposal.

While the absence of such records does not conclusively disprove every concern Carden raised, the records produced did not substantiate his specific public characterization of the meeting proposal.

Carden also stated in August correspondence that the Council's ordinance did not authorize members to conduct audits or exercise independent oversight and enforcement powers.

GAAA responded that, to its knowledge, members were not independently conducting audits or exercising enforcement authority. They were considering whether to recommend changes for the Board to evaluate.

GAAA asked Carden to identify the specific Council conduct he believed exceeded its authority and asked whether County Legal had advised members of any alleged violation during a public meeting.

County Attorney Jenny Carter declined to provide legal opinions to residents, explaining that County attorneys represent Gwinnett County.

GAAA subsequently narrowed its question to whether such a warning had ever been given during a public meeting. The correspondence in GAAA's evidence file contains no further response answering that factual question.

Different Explanations for the Same Decision

Carden's public allegations raised another question: how did they relate to the County's original explanation for restructuring the Council?

Before the August 4 vote, the County had prepared an information sheet addressing anticipated questions.

One question asked whether the restructuring was prompted by concerns about the current Council.

The County's prepared answer was: "No."

The written justification instead emphasized modernization, professional expertise, and improved coordination and collaboration.

Yet Carden subsequently cited alleged misconduct and members acting beyond their authority as reasons for reform.

Commissioner Ben Ku later offered another explanation for the abrupt timing, stating that staff-safety concerns contributed to the suddenness of the restructuring.

GAAA sought records concerning a referenced threat involving shelter staff. Community Services Director Lindsey Jorstad confirmed that the individual associated with the threat was not an Animal Advisory Council member.

Records later identified the referenced incident as a March 17, 2026 threat associated with a dispute involving a dog named Stella.

The County also reported finding no responsive communications from Jorstad during the requested June 1 through August 4 period connecting that incident to the Council, its members or meetings, or the restructuring.

These findings do not establish that safety concerns played no role. They do leave the documented connection between the incident and the restructuring unresolved.

Taken together, the County's prepared explanation, Carden's subsequent allegations, and Ku's statements raise questions about what prompted the decision and why it was handled when it was.

The August 4 Decision: Prepared in Advance, Added That Day

Records obtained through Open Records Act requests established that the restructuring package had been substantively developed by July 31, four days before the Board's vote.

Community Services Director Lindsey Jorstad submitted a justification memorandum, resolution, and replacement ordinance for the August 4 Business Session.

The proposal reduced the Council from seven members to five specialized seats and eliminated the previous district-based citizen appointment structure.

Under the new arrangement, the Community Services Director recommends candidates for the specialized seats, and the Board makes the appointments. The Animal Welfare & Enforcement Director serves as the Council's nonvoting chair.

Because Animal Welfare & Enforcement operates within Community Services, GAAA has raised concerns about the advisory body's independence under the new structure.

Despite the July 31 submission, the restructuring was absent from the advance public agenda.

On the morning of August 4, Jorstad emailed commissioners about adding the item to that afternoon's 2 p.m. Business Session.

During the meeting, Commissioner Jasper Watkins III moved to add GCID 2026-1079 to the agenda. The motion passed 4–1, with Commissioner Ben Ku opposed.

The Board then approved the restructuring by a 5–0 vote. Public comment followed.

According to the complaint, no intervening event or reason establishing the necessity for same-day consideration was identified at the meeting.

Ku later stated that he opposed adding the item because he wanted more time to consider it.

This sequence became central to the lawsuit's Open Meetings Act claim.

Georgia law generally requires an advance agenda to include matters expected to come before a public agency.

Although the law allows certain matters to be added during a meeting, the plaintiffs allege that the restructuring was already an expected agenda item and therefore should have been publicly listed.

The Records the Public Still Hasn't Seen

After the vote, GAAA sought to establish a basic timeline: who initiated the restructuring, when the proposal originated, and how it developed before reaching the Board.

The County produced the July 31 agenda materials, confirming that the proposal was already developed before August 4.

But those records did not answer who first proposed the restructuring or when that process began.

GAAA submitted a narrower records request seeking only information sufficient to identify the date and person or persons involved in initiating the restructuring.

The request expressly excluded the substance of privileged legal advice.

The County acknowledged that responsive records existed but withheld them in full, citing attorney-client privilege.

GAAA asked the County to review the records individually and release any reasonably separable nonprivileged portions.

The County maintained its position.

Other records requests also encountered privilege claims, including for additional drafts, redlines, and communications concerning the restructuring.

In one separate instance, GAAA successfully obtained identifying information about an email after narrowing its request to exclude the email's substantive content.

The County released the date, sender, recipients, and subject information while continuing to withhold the body.

That example matters because Georgia's Open Records Act generally requires nonexempt portions of records to be released when they can be reasonably separated from exempt information.

GAAA is not arguing that legitimate attorney-client communications must be disclosed.

The question is whether every record withheld in full qualifies for the claimed exemption and whether nonexempt factual information must be released.

The complaint asks the Court to examine those withholdings privately and determine whether the County properly applied the exemptions.

Did the Restructuring Legally End the Existing Appointments?

A separate legal question arose immediately after the August 4 vote.

At 3:01 p.m., Jorstad notified a sitting Council member that the terms of all current members had concluded as part of the transition.

Other members received similar notices that their appointments had ended.

But according to the complaint, neither the restructuring resolution nor the replacement ordinance expressly terminated the sitting members' appointments.

The meeting minutes also do not reflect a separate Board vote removing those members.

The replacement ordinance includes provisions concerning removal of members by majority vote of the Board.

Two former Council members now challenge whether their appointments were legally terminated through the restructuring and subsequent staff notices.

The claim does not dispute the Board's general authority to change an advisory council's structure. It asks the Court to determine the legal effect of the specific action taken on August 4.

What the Lawsuit Asks the Court to Decide

The September 30 lawsuit brings three distinct legal challenges arising from these events.

1. Georgia Open Meetings Act

Whether the County violated advance agenda requirements by adding and approving a restructuring proposal that had been prepared and formally submitted days before the meeting.

The plaintiffs seek appropriate declaratory and injunctive relief, including a determination of whether the August 4 action is legally binding.

2. Georgia Open Records Act

Whether the County properly withheld records under attorney-client privilege and attorney work product, and whether any reasonably separable nonexempt information must be produced.

The complaint seeks an in camera review, allowing the judge to examine the withheld records privately before deciding whether disclosure is required.

3. Council Appointments

Whether the August 4 action lawfully terminated two sitting Council members' appointments, or whether subsequent staff notices treated those appointments as ended without sufficient Board action.

The affected former members seek a judicial declaration and, if warranted, restoration to the Council.

These are claims and remedies requested by the plaintiffs. The allegations have not been adjudicated.

Why This Matters to Gwinnett Residents

The controversy surrounding the Animal Advisory Council is about more than the membership of one advisory body.

For years, animal advocates have asked Gwinnett County to confront longstanding problems and pursue prevention-focused solutions.

By July 2026, several Council members were bringing those issues into public discussions and developing potential recommendations for commissioners.

The Board's decision to restructure the Council interrupted that work under the existing structure.

County officials subsequently offered explanations involving modernization, Council conduct, and staff safety.

Public records established that the restructuring had been prepared in advance, while records identifying its earliest origins remained withheld.

The lawsuit now asks the Court to determine whether the County complied with legal requirements governing public meetings, access to records, and Council appointments.

Regardless of the outcome, those questions are important to every resident who expects government decisions to be made through a transparent and lawful process.

What began as growing demands for transparency and accountability in Gwinnett animal welfare has now raised questions about transparency and accountability in the County's own decision-making.

GAAA will continue following the case and advocating for meaningful animal welfare improvements, public participation, and government accountability.
