Gwinnett County Faces Lawsuit Over Animal Advisory Council Restructuring, Public Meeting Notice, and Withheld Records

October 15, 2026 · Gwinnett Animal Advocacy Alliance

On September 30, 2026, three individuals, including two former members of Gwinnett County's Animal Advisory Council, filed a lawsuit against Gwinnett County in Superior Court.

The lawsuit raises questions about the County's August 4 decision to dissolve and restructure its Animal Advisory Council, the process used to bring that decision before commissioners, the withholding of public records, and whether two Council members' appointments were lawfully terminated.

The Gwinnett County Sheriff's Office served the County on October 7, 2026.

This lawsuit is about more than the restructuring of an advisory council. It raises fundamental questions about public notice, government transparency, and the legal process used to end citizen appointments.

What Happened on August 4?

Before August 4, Gwinnett County's Animal Advisory Council consisted of seven members, including citizens appointed to represent individual commission districts.

Council members had been examining animal welfare issues, shelter operations, and potential recommendations for improvement, including work related to a countywide spay/neuter voucher program.

Public participation was growing. More than 50 residents attended the Council's July 13 meeting, and a special meeting was scheduled for September 3 to continue unfinished discussions.

Yet on August 4, the Board of Commissioners voted unanimously to repeal and replace the Council's governing ordinance, creating a new five-member, skills-based structure.

The restructuring was not listed on the advance public agenda.

According to the complaint and referenced County records, the proposal had already been developed and formally submitted through the County's agenda process on July 31, four days before the meeting.

How the Restructuring Unfolded

  1. July 13 — Residents Attend Council Meeting

    More than 50 residents attended the Animal Advisory Council meeting. Additional work remained, and a special September 3 meeting was scheduled.

  2. July 31 — Restructuring Proposal Submitted

    The County's proposal, including a justification memorandum, resolution, and replacement ordinance, was formally submitted for the August 4 meeting, according to the complaint.

  3. August 4, 10:27 a.m. — Request to Add Agenda Item

    Community Services Director Lindsey Jorstad emailed commissioners requesting that the restructuring be added to the 2 p.m. meeting agenda.

  4. August 4 — Commissioners Vote

    Commissioner Jasper Watkins III moved to add the item to the agenda. The motion passed 4–1, with Commissioner Ben Ku opposed.

    The Board then approved the restructuring itself by a 5–0 vote. Public comment occurred after the vote.

  5. After August 4 — Members Notified Their Terms Ended

    Sitting Council members received notifications indicating that their terms had concluded.

  6. September 30 — Lawsuit Filed

    Three individuals filed suit in Gwinnett County Superior Court, challenging aspects of the restructuring process, records withholding, and Council appointments.

  7. October 7 — Gwinnett County Served

    The Gwinnett County Sheriff's Office served the County with the lawsuit.

1. Was Georgia's Open Meetings Act Violated?

Georgia's Open Meetings Act requires public agencies to make available an agenda identifying matters expected to come before them. The law also permits certain matters to be added during a meeting when they become necessary to address during that meeting.

The complaint alleges that the restructuring was already an expected agenda item because the proposal had been prepared and formally submitted days before the August 4 meeting.

It further alleges that no intervening event or explanation establishing the need for same-day consideration was identified during the meeting.

Despite the proposal's advance preparation, it was added to the agenda only after the August 4 meeting began.

Public comment took place after commissioners had already voted.

If the County had prepared the restructuring days earlier, why wasn't it included on the advance public agenda?

The plaintiffs argue that the omission deprived residents and Council members of advance notice and a meaningful opportunity to participate before the decision was made.

They ask the Court to determine whether the County violated Georgia's Open Meetings Act and whether the August 4 action should be declared nonbinding to the extent authorized by law.

2. Why Are Records About the Restructuring Being Withheld?

Following the August 4 vote, Open Records Act requests sought to establish how the restructuring originated and developed.

Among the questions were:

  • Who first proposed or requested the restructuring?
  • When did discussions about changing the Council begin?
  • When were the first drafts prepared?
  • What records document how the proposal moved through the County's decision-making process?

According to the complaint, the County acknowledged that responsive records existed concerning when the restructuring was first initiated and who initiated it.

However, the County withheld those records in their entirety, citing attorney-client privilege.

Additional drafts, redlines, and related records were withheld under claims of attorney-client privilege or attorney work product.

The lawsuit does not argue that legitimate attorney-client communications must be disclosed.

Instead, the plaintiffs request that the Court examine the withheld materials privately through a process known as in camera review.

The purpose is to determine whether the claimed exemptions were properly applied and whether nonexempt portions, including factual, identifying, or administrative information, must be produced.

The complaint also describes an instance in which the County released identifying information about an email while continuing to withhold its body as privileged.

The plaintiffs cite that production as an example of why other withheld records warrant closer review.

The question is not whether the County can protect legitimate legal advice. It is whether all the information being withheld is legally exempt from public disclosure.

3. Were Two Council Members Lawfully Removed?

The third claim concerns two former Animal Advisory Council members who served as the District 1 and District 2 appointees.

Neither had resigned before the August 4 restructuring.

After commissioners approved the replacement ordinance, County staff notified sitting Council members that their terms had concluded.

However, the complaint alleges that neither the resolution nor the replacement ordinance expressly terminated the existing members' appointments.

The complaint also notes that the August 4 meeting minutes do not reflect a separate Board vote removing the sitting Council members.

The replacement ordinance contains provisions addressing removal of members by majority vote of the Board of Commissioners.

Did the Board actually terminate the members' appointments, or did County staff subsequently treat those appointments as terminated without the necessary Board action?

The plaintiffs ask the Court to determine the legal effect of the August 4 vote and the subsequent notices.

If the Court determines that the two appointments were not lawfully terminated, the lawsuit seeks appropriate relief, including restoration of those appointments.

The County's Explanations Have Also Raised Questions

The complaint describes differences between the County's prepared explanation before the vote and statements made afterward.

Before the August 4 meeting, the County prepared an information sheet addressing anticipated questions about the restructuring.

One question asked:

"Is this because of concerns with the current Council?"

The County's prepared answer: "No."

The accompanying explanation characterized the restructuring as a periodic review reflecting Gwinnett's growth and evolving community needs.

Afterward, Commissioner Kirkland Carden publicly raised concerns about Council members allegedly exceeding their authority and violating public meeting laws.

Commissioner Ben Ku later cited staff safety concerns as contributing to the suddenness of the restructuring.

According to the complaint, records obtained concerning a referenced March 2026 threat identified an individual who was not a member of the Animal Advisory Council.

The County also reported having no responsive communications, within the requested period, connecting that threat to the Council or the restructuring.

These differences raise questions about the explanation for the decision and the timeline leading up to it.

On October 15, The North Gwinnett Voice published an investigative report examining County records, the August 4 timeline, and the differing explanations provided for the restructuring.

Related reporting: Read The North Gwinnett Voice investigation →

The lawsuit does not ask the Court to speculate about anyone's motives. Its legal claims concern the public meeting process, the handling of records, and the legal status of two Council appointments.

What Is the Lawsuit Asking the Court to Do?

The complaint requests several forms of relief:

  • Review the August 4 agenda process. Determine whether the County violated Georgia's Open Meetings Act by omitting an expected item from the advance public agenda.
  • Determine the legal effect of the restructuring. Consider whether the August 4 action should be declared nonbinding and whether appropriate injunctive relief is warranted.
  • Review the termination of two Council appointments. Determine whether those appointments were lawfully terminated and, if not, whether the members should be restored.
  • Examine withheld public records. Conduct a private judicial review of records withheld under attorney-client privilege and attorney work product.
  • Require production of nonexempt records. Order the release of responsive public records and reasonably separable nonexempt information where required by law.

These are remedies requested by the plaintiffs. The Court has not yet ruled on the merits of the allegations.

Why This Matters to Gwinnett Residents

This case is about more than the structure of one advisory council.

It raises questions about how local government makes decisions, how much advance notice the public receives, and whether residents can meaningfully participate before those decisions are finalized.

Citizen advisory councils exist to bring community perspectives and recommendations into government.

In Gwinnett, residents have invested considerable time raising concerns about animal welfare, shelter overcrowding, pet overpopulation, and the need for prevention-focused solutions.

When a public advisory body is restructured, the process matters.

When records about how that decision originated are requested, compliance with the Open Records Act matters.

And when citizens are appointed to serve on a public advisory body, the legal process for ending those appointments matters.

Transparency isn't simply telling residents what government decided.

It's allowing them to understand and participate in the process before the decision is made.

The three plaintiffs have asked the Court to examine these issues under Georgia law.

GAAA will continue advocating for meaningful animal welfare reform, public participation, and accountability in Gwinnett County.

The animals deserve a voice. The public deserves accountability.

About This Article & the Lawsuit

This article summarizes allegations and requests for relief contained in a lawsuit filed September 30, 2026, in Gwinnett County Superior Court.

Case number: 26-A-08947-9

The lawsuit was filed by three individuals in their personal capacities. Gwinnett Animal Advocacy Alliance (GAAA) is not a plaintiff.

The allegations have not been adjudicated, and no court has determined that Gwinnett County violated the law.

This article is intended to explain the issues raised in the public court filing, not to predict the outcome of the case.
